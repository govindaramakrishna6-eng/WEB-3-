01 The shift

Read. Write. Own.

The web has had three moods. Tap through them — the difference is all about who holds the keys.

Read

Pages you could only look at.

Websites were digital brochures. A few people published, everybody else browsed. Think GeoCities home pages, spinning “under construction” GIFs and visitor counters.

  • You were: a reader.
  • Who owned it: whoever ran the server.
  • Real example: the very first website, info.cern.ch (1991), is still online.
Read + Write

You make the content. They own the account.

Social media, YouTube, marketplaces: anyone can post. But your followers, posts and purchases live on a company’s servers, under its rules. If it bans you, changes the algorithm or shuts down, it’s gone.

  • You were: a creator — and the product.
  • Who owned it: the platform.
  • Real example: when Google shut down Google+ in 2019, users had to download what they could before it vanished.
Read + Write + Own

Your stuff lives on a public ledger, tied to your key.

Balances, names and digital items are recorded on a blockchain that no single company controls. Apps become front doors to shared data instead of walled gardens. Switch apps, keep your stuff.

  • You are: a participant with a wallet.
  • Who owns it: whoever holds the private key.
  • Real example: an ENS name like vitalik.eth works across hundreds of wallets and apps — no single one can take it away.

02 Blockchain

A notebook where every page locks the one before it.

A blockchain is a list of records (blocks). Each block contains a fingerprint (hash) of the previous one. Change one old record and every fingerprint after it stops matching — so tampering is instantly obvious.

A First, meet the fingerprint

A hash turns any text into a fixed-length code. Change a single letter and the whole thing scrambles. Try it:

SHA-256 fingerprint

Try changing 5 to 6. Same function Bitcoin uses.

Real story

A newspaper headline, sealed forever

On 3 January 2009, the anonymous creator of Bitcoin, Satoshi Nakamoto, mined the very first block. Hidden inside it is a line of text:

“The Times 03/Jan/2009 Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks”

It’s both a timestamp and a statement — and because every later block chains back to it, it can never be edited.

B Now break the chain

Each block below is valid when its hash starts with 000 and it points to the previous hash. Edit any block’s data and watch the damage spread. Then hit Mine to repair it by finding a new nonce — that’s the “work” in proof-of-work.

    03 Decentralization

    No main server. No single off switch.

    In a normal app, one company’s servers hold the truth. In a blockchain, thousands of independent computers (nodes) each keep a full copy and agree on updates. Pick a model, then start knocking computers offline.

    Click (or tap) a computer to take it offline. Click again to bring it back.

    Real story

    On 4 October 2021, a bad configuration change took Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp offline for about six hours — billions of people, one company, one mistake. Bitcoin’s ledger, run by independent nodes around the world, has kept adding blocks since 2009.

    05 Smart contracts

    A vending machine that nobody can tamper with.

    In the 1990s, computer scientist Nick Szabo compared smart contracts to vending machines: put in the right amount, get your item, no shopkeeper needed. Here’s one running step by step. Simulation · no real ETH

    1. Pick a snack

    2. Insert coins

    Sending: 0.00 ETH

    Tray is empty
    VendingMachine.sol
    contract VendingMachine {
  mapping(string => uint) price;

  function buy(string snack) payable {
    require(msg.value >= price[snack], "Not enough ETH");
    uint change = msg.value - price[snack];
    payable(msg.sender).transfer(change);
    emit Dispensed(msg.sender, snack);
  }
}

    The transaction receipt will appear here.

    Why it matters

    The rules are public code. The seller can’t quietly raise the price after you pay, and there’s no one to bribe or ask for a refund.

    Real example

    Uniswap is a set of smart contracts that swaps tokens with no company holding your money in between. It has handled well over a trillion dollars in trades.

    The catch

    Code does exactly what it says — bugs included. That’s how the 2016 DAO hack happened. “Code is law” cuts both ways.

    07 Try it for real

    Your wallet is your login.

    In Web3 you don’t sign up with an email and password. You connect a wallet, which holds a private key only you know. Connecting only shares your public address — it can’t move your funds.

    Your signature

    This proves you control the address, without revealing your key or spending gas. It’s how “Sign in with Ethereum” works.

    What just happened?

    1. The site asked your wallet for permission to see your address.
    2. You approved in the wallet pop-up. The site never sees your private key.
    3. The site read the blockchain directly to show your balance — no account database needed.
    4. Signing a message proves ownership, like a digital autograph. It’s free and doesn’t send a transaction.

    Stay safe

    • Never type your 12 or 24-word recovery phrase into any website. Ever.
    • Read every pop-up. “Sign” and “Send” are very different buttons.
    • Practice on a testnet like Sepolia — its coins are free and worthless.
